What the research says, in one view

Two qualitative HCP studies, formal advisory input, an industry benchmark of peer companies, and interviews with the internal teams who produce the content. The findings converge on one thing: HCPs are time-poor, AI-mediated, and sceptical of pharma as a teacher — but receptive to pharma as a source.

Discovery

Discovery has moved off our channels

Nearly all interviewed HCPs use AI-mediated search, often without calling it AI. The journey starts with a search or AI summary and ends at a trusted reference to verify. Being findable and citable matters more than being visited.

Trust

Trusted as a source, not as a teacher

Pharma is trusted for product information, administration and device training, and access support. Pharma-led formal education and CME-style modules are consistently rejected as a primary learning source.

Format

Visual structure beats narrative

Bullets, flow diagrams, charts and short navigable video outperform prose. Long-form text is described as too slow to scan and “saved for later, often never revisited”.

Content

Congress recaps lead the wanted list

Concise congress recaps and trial summaries were the single most attractive proposition tested — ranked first by both physicians and pharmacists.

Concept testing

Five behaviour modes

The newer research designs around what an HCP is trying to achieve rather than their job title. One HCP moves between modes across a single day, so the platform must serve the mode, not the persona.

ModePriorityWhat they need
Ad-hoc information searchersHigh Quick, reliable answers in the flow of work. Brief engagement; clarity, credibility and immediate relevance.
Evidence explorersHigh Go beyond the quick answer — follow citations, revisit between moments, work through how evidence applies.
Scientific interpretersImplicated Rigour, transparency and access to primary data. Largely served by content built for the two priority modes.
Patient-facing translatorsLower Reusable, plain-language material to support patient conversations. Around two-thirds of the priority modes also want this.
Mastery-driven learnersDe-prioritise Structured formal learning over time — but they seek it from independent bodies and are sceptical of pharma providing it.
Strategic shift: from deep, planned learning to in-the-moment clinical utility and decision support.

Preferences by profession

Earlier concept-testing research segmented by role. The three profiles want the same qualities — brevity, evidence, neutrality — but weight them differently, and one wants the opposite of oversimplification.

ProfessionLearning modeWhat they want
PhysiciansGoal-driven and exploratory Powerful search and filter; short scannable formats with progressive depth; references and evidence visible; fast loading with key insights up front.
PharmacistsGoal-driven, not exploratory Clinical decision support first; direct access to prescribing information, dosing and trial data; facts over storytelling. Low tolerance for AI recommendations — summaries are acceptable.
Nurse practitionersDuty-driven, “not leisurely” Layered depth, explicitly not oversimplified; save-for-later and visible progress; source and authorship transparency; formats they can reuse when teaching their team.
Note the tension: the same research that asks for brevity also records HCPs who reject summaries outright — “I don’t want bite-sized blurbs, I want the study.” Progressive depth is how both are served: a scannable surface with the full evidence one click away.

Content and formats

What to make, and the constraint that decides how to make it.

Wanted most

Congress recaps and trial summaries · “key takeaways” framing · concise clinical data and publications · pipeline information · short navigable video · infographics and one-page assets · plain-language patient-ready summaries.

Wanted least

Long-form educational journeys · CME-style modules from pharma · thought leadership framed as learning · dense prose · anything requiring registration before showing value.

The constraint that shapes everything: the formats HCPs most want — short video and infographics — are not readable by the AI systems that increasingly mediate discovery. Every visual asset needs a text twin: a transcript, caption or structured summary. Without it, content wins the preference and loses the audience.

How trust is earned and lost

Trust is composite, fragile, and asymmetric — slow to build and immediate to lose.

Earned by

Three pillars

Reputation and longevity — established source, publisher standing, peer regard.
Accuracy — peer review, evidence base, references listed, clear methodology.
Specificity — dedicated to medical use, and free of advertising.

Branding rule

Disclose, don’t lead

HCPs do not want sponsor branding front and centre, but they do want honesty about the source. A subtle, upfront line — “this platform is supported by …” — strikes the balance. Zero brand push.

Lost by

One or two strikes

Outputs that feel authoritative without evidence, cite unfamiliar sources, or over-reach into clinical reasoning. At least half of interviewed HCPs stopped using a tool after one or two such experiences.

Implication

Medical vs commercial

Most HCPs cannot distinguish medical from commercial communication and do not consider the distinction relevant — they see information and a company name. Effort is better spent on usefulness than on the label.

The preferences that change what we build

Filtered from the full set to those corroborated by more than one independent source, that change what gets built or measured, and that fit the scientific-engagement positioning.

  1. Structured, citable, machine-readable content as the defaultDiscovery is AI-mediated. Being referenced and trusted by those systems matters more than being visited.
  2. Every visual asset ships with a text twinThe cheapest high-leverage fix available: the most-wanted formats are the ones machines cannot read.
  3. Measure share of answer, not pageviewsVisibility, citation and decision-support usage. Peer companies are still measuring visits.
  4. Design for ad-hoc searchers and evidence explorersThe highest-frequency, highest-value modes, and the ones aligned to AI discovery.
  5. Authenticate to unlock depth, not basic informationHCPs want tailored formats without handing over personal details. One rule, applied per content type.
  6. Visual structure over narrativeBullets, diagrams, charts and short video. Prose is scanned and abandoned.
  7. Build for the search-then-verify journeyHCPs start with a search or AI summary and move to a trusted reference to confirm. Be present in both steps.
  8. Treat trust as one-strikeAccuracy monitoring is a retention requirement, not a compliance chore.
  9. Congress recaps and trial summaries as the lead content typeThe only proposition tested that ranked first with both physicians and pharmacists.
  10. Value inside two minutes, depth opt-inNever required, always available. “Be brief, be bright, be gone” — with a route to the detail.
  11. Non-promotional, with subtle upfront disclosureCheap to get right; expensive to get wrong given one-strike trust.
  12. Lead with interpretation and a bottom lineInterpretation is remembered even when figures are not.
  13. Self-directed navigation, no forced sequenceBehavioural evidence: a mandatory learning path was built internally and abandoned because HCPs wanted to self-direct.

Demoted by the positioning decision

CME credit and CME-style modules · curriculum engine and completion tracking · long-form educational journeys · “medical education” as the framing.

Promoted by it

The patient-ready depth layer — plain-language summaries and printable assets. Around two-thirds of the priority modes want it, and it sits in the area HCPs already trust pharma for.

Method, and what is not proven

The evidence is credible and directional. It is not statistically powered, and the summary above is honest about which parts are strong.
